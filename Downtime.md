- *Downtime Activities in the Dungeon Master's Guide (2014) Chapter 6*

Downtime activities are tasks that are done between adventures. These tasks can include buying or creating magic items, pulling off crimes, and working at a job. A character selects a downtime activity from among those available and pays the cost of that activity in time and money. The results of the activity are determined by the DM and also often by dice rolls.

Downtime typically gets handled outside of play sessions through online messaging.

# Activity Rules
This section describes the specific rules for each downtime activity. These include descriptions of the activity, costs, resolutions, and potential complications. Unless specified otherwise, the player gets to make all rolls related to the resolution of the activity while the DM makes all rolls related to complications. Time is often referred to in workweeks and days in each activity. One workweek is equal to five days (usually accompanied by 2 days of rest). One day is equal to 8 hours of work related to the activity being done.

## Bastion Construction
Characters can design and build new additions or changes to their bastion during downtime.

***Cost.*** Costs are equal to the costs of adding or renovating facilities in the Dungeon Master's Guide (2024) chapter 8.

***Complications.*** Bastion construction can have a diverse set of complications. Every completed renovation brings a 10 percent chance of a complication, examples of which are listed in the Bastion Construction Complications table.

**Bastion Construction Complications**
| d10 | Complication |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Shoddy craftsmanship has led to part of your bastion breaking or collapsing. It will require 1d10 x 10 GP to fix.
| 2 | The latest work done to your bastion has revealed that it's haunted.
| 3 | The construction is complete, but the workers took some creative liberty and it doesn't quite look like you wanted it to...
| 4 | A lord claims that your bastion is on their land.
| 5 | A crazy gnome has taken up residence in your bastion who is causing trouble and you can't find a way to get them to leave.
| 6 | Some new kind of dangerous mold or bug has begun popping up in your bastion since you finished construction.
| 7 | A pixie becomes angry that your bastion is blocking its view of something and is taking it out on you in seemingly immature ways.
| 8 | Local authorities accuse you of illegal activities in your bastion and want to search it, but you know that they'll destroy the place in doing so.
| 9 | Something, such as a plant growing under your bastion or termites, is causing damage to your newly constructed / renovated facility.
| 10 | You discover that one of the workers who helped with your bastion was a spy!

## Buying a Magic Item
Purchasing a magic item requires time and money to seek out and contact people willing to sell items. Even then, there is no guarantee a seller will have the items a character desires.

***Cost.*** Finding magic items to purchase requires at least one workweek of effort and 100 gp in expenses. Spending more time and money increases your chance of finding a high-quality item as described below.

***Resolution.*** A character seeking to buy a magic item makes a Charisma (Persuasion) check to determine the quality of the seller found. The character gains a +1 bonus on the check for every workweek beyond the first that is spent seeking a seller and a +1 bonus for every additional 100 gp spent on the search, up to a maximum bonus of +10. The monetary cost for each additional workweek includes a wealthy lifestyle, for a buyer must impress potential business partners.

As shown on the Buying Magic Items table, the total of the check dictates which table in the Dungeon Master's Guide (2024) to roll on to determine which items are on the market. The DM makes the rolls to determine these magic items. Alternatively, you can choose to have the DM roll for items from any table associated with a lower check total on the Buying Magic Items table.

**Buying Magic Items Table**
| Check Total | Items Acquired |
| --- | --- |
| 1-10 | 1d12 Common items |
| 11-20 | 1d10 Uncommon items |
| 21-25 | 1d10 Rare items |
| 26-30 | 1d10 Very Rare items |
| 31+ | 1d8 Legendary items |

The player can specify a magic item they are looking for to the DM. If they do so before they make the check and the rarity of the table they roll is equal to or exceeds the rarity of the item they're looking for, then that item will be included as one of the items for sale. For example, if a character wants a rare item and their roll gets them items from the rare table or higher, the DM can make that item one of the ones for sale from this activity.

Once the player sees which magic items are for sale, they can choose which ones they're interested in buying. To determine their prices, the player can haggle by making a Charisma (Persuasion or Deception) check for each item. Use the Magic Item Base Prices table and the Magic Item Offer table to determine the offer price. The DM makes the rolls to determine the final price of each item.

**Magic Item Base Prices**
| Rarity | Base Price* |
| --- | --- |
| Common | 100 GP |
| Uncommon | 400 GP |
| Rare | 4,000 GP |
| Very Rare | 40,000 GP |
| Legendary | 200,000 GP |

\*Be sure to add the cost of non-magical versions of items to the total price.

**Magic Item Offer**
| Check Total | Offer |
| --- | --- |
| 1—10 | 100 + 1d8 x 10 percent of base price |
| 11—20 | 90 + 1d20 percent of base price |
| 21+ | 10 + 1d8 x 10 percent of base price |

***Complications.*** The magic item trade is fraught with peril. The large sums of money involved and the power offered by magic items attract thieves, con artists, and other villains. Every workweek spent trying to buy a magic item brings a 10 percent chance of a complication, examples of which are on the Magic Item Purchase Complications table.

**Magic Item Purchase Complications**
| d12 | Complication |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | The item is a fake, planted by an enemy. |
| 2 | The item is stolen by the party's enemies. |
| 3 | The item is cursed by a god. |
| 4 | The item's original owner will kill to reclaim it; the party's enemies spread news of its sale. |
| 5 | The item is at the center of a dark prophecy. |
| 6 | The seller is murdered before the sale. |
| 7 | The seller is a devil looking to make a bargain. |

## Carousing
Carousing is a default downtime activity for many characters. Between adventures, who doesn't want to relax with a few drinks and a group of friends at a tavern?

***Cost.*** Carousing covers a workweek of fine food, strong drink, and socializing. A character can attempt to carouse among lower-, middle-, or upper-class folk. A character can carouse with the lower class for 10 GP to cover expenses, or 50 GP for the middle class. Carousing with the upper class requires 250 GP for the workweek and access to the local nobility.

A character with the noble background can mingle with the upper class, but other characters can do so only if you judge that the character has made sufficient contacts. Alternatively, a character might use a disguise kit and the Deception skill to pass as a noble visiting from a distant city.

***Resolution.*** After a workweek of carousing, a character stands to make contacts within the selected social class. The character makes a Charisma (Persuasion) check using the Carousing table.

**Carousing**
| Check Total | Result |
| --- | --- |
| 1-5 | Character has made a hostile contact. |
| 6-10 | Character has made no new contacts. |
| 11-15 | Character has made an allied contact. |
| 16-20 | Character has made two allied contacts. |
| 21+ | Character has made three allied contacts. |

Contacts are NPCs who now share a bond with the character. Each one either owes the character a favor or has some reason to bear a grudge. A hostile contact works against the character, placing obstacles but stopping short of committing a crime or a violent act. Allied contacts are friends who will render aid to the character, but not at the risk of their lives.

Lower-class contacts include criminals, laborers, mercenaries, the town guard, and any other folk who normally frequent the cheapest taverns in town.

Middle-class contacts include guild members, spellcasters, town officials, and other folk who frequent well-kept establishments.

Upper-class contacts are nobles and their personal servants. Carousing with such folk covers formal banquets, state dinners, and the like.

Once a contact has helped or hindered a character, the character needs to carouse again to get back into the NPC's good graces. A contact provides help once, not help for life. The contact remains friendly, which can influence roleplaying and how the characters interact with them, but doesn't come with a guarantee of help.

Players choose NPCs they've met in the area to become allied contacts. Alternatively, the player can forgo this decision to allow the DM to assign an NPC as an allied contact. When a hostile contact is made, the DM decides which NPC becomes that contact.

At any time, a character can have a maximum number of allied contacts equal to 1 + the character's Charisma modifier (minimum of 1). A character cannot gain anymore allied contacts once this limit has been reached.

***Complications.*** Characters who carouse risk bar brawls, accumulating a cloud of nasty rumors, and building a bad reputation around town. A character has a 10 percent chance of triggering a complication for each workweek of carousing.

## Crafting an Item
A character with the right resources can craft their own items, either magical or non-magical.

***Cost.*** Crafting an item requires time, raw materials, tools, and proficiency with those tools. Characters meeting these requirements can combine the needed raw materials using the correct tools to create non-magical items. To create magical items, characters can turn a non-magical item magical, whether it was crafted by them or not, by using the same process.

To determine the time required to make a non-magical item, divide its gold piece cost by 10. For example, you need 5 days to make a Heavy Crossbow, which sells for 50 GP. The time required to turn a non-magical item magical is determined by its rarity. The Magic Item Crafting Time and Cost table shows these time requirements. This time is halved for consumable magic items, such as potions. Multiple characters can combine their efforts to reduce the crafting time of an item. Divide the normal crafting time by the number of characters working on it to determine how long it would take to craft the item with that many characters. The DM can impose limits for how many characters can be working on an item at the same time. For example, only up to 2 characters could work on something small like a ring at the same time, while something larger could take up to 4 or more characters. Brewing potions of healing has different time requirements than other items. See the Potion of Healing Creation table below to determine their time costs.

To find the cost of the raw materials needed to create a non-magic item, divide the item's normal purchase cost in half. For example, you need 750 GP of raw materials to make Plate Armor, which sells for 1,500 GP. The cost of the raw materials required to imbue an item with magic to turn it magical is determined by its rarity. The Magic Item Crafting Time and Cost table shows these cost requirements. This cost is halved for consumable magic items, such as potions. Brewing potions of healing has different raw material requirements than other items. See the Potion of Healing Creation table below to determine their material costs.

**Magic Item Crafting Time and Cost**
| Item Rarity | Time | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common | 5 days | 50 GP |
| Uncommon | 10 days | 200 GP |
| Rare | 50 days | 2,000 GP |
| Very Rare | 125 days | 20,000 GP |
| Legendary | 250 days | 100,000 GP |

**Potion of Healing Creation**
| Type | Time | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Healing | 1 day | 25 gp |
| Greater healing | 1 workweek | 100 gp |
| Superior healing | 3 workweeks | 1,000 gp |
| Supreme healing | 4 workweeks | 10,000 gp |

The DM determines whether appropriate raw materials are available. In a city, there is a 75 percent chance that the materials are available, and in any other settlement, that chance is 25 percent. If materials aren't available, you must wait at least 7 days before checking on the availability again.

The raw material cost of turning an item magical can sometimes be replaced by tracking down and collecting exotic materials. For example, a magic item could be created from the hide of a Yeti and / or a vial of water taken from a whirlpool in the Elemental Plane of Water. Doing this requires a recipe, which can be obtained from research or as treasure. It's also typically done as part of an adventure. The DM determines whether this method is possible for a certain magic item, how the recipe can be obtained, and what the required material would be. The Magic Item Ingredients table suggests the challenge rating of a creature that the characters need to face to acquire the materials for an item. Note that facing a creature does not necessarily mean that the characters must collect items from its corpse. Rather, the creature might guard a location or a resource that the characters need access to.

**Magic Item Ingredients**
| Item Rarity | CR Range |
| --- | --- |
| Common | 1—3 |
| Uncommon | 4—8 |
| Rare | 9—12 |
| Very rare | 13—18 |
| Legendary | 19+ |

Having access to certain tools is also required for crafting items. To determine what kinds of tools are required to craft certain items, see the Tools section in chapter 6 of the Player's Handbook (2024) and find a tool that crafts the item the character wants to craft. If no tool lists the desired item, the DM determines what tool is required to craft the item. All characters involved in crafting an item must also be proficient with the tools needed to craft that item. Having access to and proficiency with the correct tools to craft a non-magical item is also required to turn that item magical. Additionally, all characters involved with turning an item magical must have proficiency with the Arcana skill.

***Complications.*** Most of the complications involved in creating something, especially a magic item, are linked to the difficulty in finding rare ingredients or components needed to complete the work. The complications a character might face as byproducts of the creation process are most interesting when the characters are working on a magic item: there's a 10 percent chance for every five workweeks spent on crafting an item that a complication occurs. The Crafting Complications table provides examples of what might happen.

**Crafting Complications**
| d6 | Complication |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Rumors swirl that what you're working on is unstable and a threat to the community. |
| 2 | Your tools are stolen, forcing you to buy new ones. |
| 3 | A local wizard shows keen interest in your work and insists on observing you. |
| 4 | A powerful noble offers a hefty price for your work and is not interested in hearing no for an answer. |
| 5 | A dwarf clan accuses you of stealing its secret lore to fuel your work. |
| 6 | A competitor spreads rumors that your work is shoddy and prone to failure. |

## Crime
Sometimes it pays to be bad. This activity gives a character the chance to make some extra cash, at the risk of arrest.

***Cost.*** A character must spend 1 workweek and at least 25 GP gathering information on potential targets before committing the intended crime.

***Resolution.*** The character must make a series of checks, with the DC for all the checks chosen by the character according to the amount of profit sought from the crime.

The chosen DC can be 10, 15, 20, or 25. Successful completion of the crime yields a number of gold pieces, as shown on the Loot Value table. The gold pieces are either from robbing a target or being paid by a client who hired you to do a crime.

Alternatively, a player can choose to replace some or all of the gold pieces with specific items if they want to steal things other than coins. They can be standard non-magical items such as adventuring gear, or they can be magic items from a seller that a character has tracked down from the [Buying a Magic Item](#buying-a-magic-item) activity. The DM determines whether or not these items are available to steal. To determine how many gold pieces the character will earn in addition to the items if a character chooses to do this, subtract the total cost of the items from the GP in the Reward Value table based on the chosen DC. The total cost of the items cannot exceed the GP value for the chosen DC.

To attempt a crime, the character makes three checks: Dexterity (Stealth), Dexterity (sleight of hand) using thieves' tools, and the player's choice of Intelligence (Investigation), Wisdom (Perception), or Charisma (Deception).

If none of the checks are successful, the character is caught and jailed. The character must pay a fine equal to the profit the crime would have earned and must spend one week in jail for each 25 GP of the fine.

If only one check is successful, the crime fails but the character escapes.

If two checks are successful, the crime is a partial success, netting the character half the payout. This could be from losing some of the loot when stealing or not meeting all the requests from a client when vandalizing, kidnapping, or assassinating. If the player chose to rob items, the DM determines which items the character kept and lost.

If all three checks are successful, the character earns the full value of the reward.

**Reward Value**
| DC | Value | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 50 GP | Robbery of a struggling merchant, vandalizing a lower-class person's home, etc. |
| 15 | 100 GP | Robbery of a prosperous merchant, kidnapping a middle-class person, etc. |
| 20 | 200 GP | Robbery of a noble, assassinating an upper-class person, etc. |
| 25 | 1,000 GP | Robbery of one of the richest figures in town, vandalizing an important project of a powerful wizard, etc. |

***Complications.*** A life of crime is filled with complications. Roll on the Crime Complications table if the character succeeds on only one check.

**Crime Complications**
| d8 | Complication
| --- | --- |
| 1 | A bounty equal to your earnings is offered for information about your crime. |
| 2 | An unknown person contacts you, threatening to reveal your crime if you don't render a service. |
| 3 | Your victim or people close to your victim are financially ruined by your crime. |
| 4 | Someone who knows of your crime has been arrested on an unrelated matter. |
| 5 | Your loot is a stolen single, easily identified item that you can't fence in this region. |
| 6 | Your crime was against someone who was under a local crime lord's protection, and who now wants revenge. |
| 7 | Your victim or people close to your victim call in a favor from a guard, doubling the efforts to solve the case. |
| 8 | Your victim asks or people close to your victim ask one of your adventuring companions to solve the crime. |

## Gaining Renown
A character can undertake minor tasks for or socialize with people in an organization or community to improve their standing with them.

***Cost.*** A character needs to have a renown score of at least 1 with an organization or community to be able to improve their renown during downtime. It takes a number of workweeks equal to 2 times the character's current renown score to improve their renown.

***Resolution.*** Once the required time has been spent, the character's renown score increases by 1 point.

***Complications.*** Complications that arise from working for an organization or socializing with its members typically come from social interactions that go wrong. Every 2 workweeks spent gaining renown brings a 10 percent chance of complications, examples of which are in the Gaining Renown Complications table.

**Gaining Renown Complications**
| d6 | Complication |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | You offended the leader of the organization. |
| 2 | A rumor is spreading that you're a sympathizer toward something that's a great threat to the organization / community. Whether that rumor is true or not is up to you. |
| 3 | You messed up a job and people have a little less faith in you now. |
| 4 | You're getting pressured to take on a risky task. |
| 5 | A hazing incident from some higher-ups leaves you a little scared. |
| 6 | You discover a member who's been giving you a lot of support in the organization is a fiend.

## Gambling
Games of chance are a way to make a fortune— and perhaps a better way to lose one.

***Cost.*** This activity requires one workweek of effort plus a stake of at least 10 GP, to a maximum of 1,000 GP or more if the DM allows it.

***Resolution.*** The character must make a series of checks, with a DC determined at random based on the quality of the competition that the character runs into. Part of the risk of gambling is that one never knows who might end up sitting across the table.

The character makes three checks: Wisdom (Insight), Charisma (Deception), and Charisma (Intimidation). If the character has proficiency with an appropriate gaming set, that tool proficiency can replace the relevant skill in any of the checks. The DC for each of the checks is 5 + 2d10; generate a separate DC for each one. Consult the Gambling Results table to see how the character did.

**Gambling Results**
| Result | Value |
| --- | --- |
| 0 successes | Lose all the money you bet, and accrue a debt equal to that amount. |
| 1 success | Lose half the money you bet. |
| 2 successes | Gain the amount you bet plus half again more. |
| 3 successes | Gain double the amount you bet. |

***Complications.*** Gambling tends to attract unsavory individuals. The potential complications involved come from run-ins with the law and associations with various criminals tied to the activity. Every workweek spent gambling brings a 10 percent chance of a complication, examples of which are on the Gambling Complications table.

**Gambling Complications**
| d6 | Complication |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | You are accused of cheating. You decide whether you actually did cheat or were framed. |
| 2 | The town guards raid the gambling hall and throw you in jail. |
| 3 | A noble in town loses badly to you and loudly vows to get revenge. |
| 4 | You won a sum from a low-ranking member of a thieves' guild, and the guild wants its money back. |
| 5 | A local crime boss insists you start frequenting the boss's gambling parlor and no others. |
| 6 | A high-stakes gambler comes to town and insists that you take part in a game. |

## Performing Sacred Rites
A pious character can spend time between adventures performing sacred rites in a temple affiliated with a god they revere. Between rites, the character spends time in meditation and prayer. A character who is a priest in the temple can lead these rites, which might include weddings, funerals, and ordinations. A layperson can offer sacrifices in a temple or assist a priest with a rite.

***Cost.*** Performing sacred rites requires one workweek of time from a character.

***Resolution.*** A character who spends a workweek performing sacred rites gains Heroic Inspiration at the start of each day for the next 2d6 days.

***Complications.*** Complications from performing sacred rites typically arise from the rite going wrong in some way. Every workweek of performing sacred rites brings a 5 percent chance of complications, examples of which are listed in the Sacred Rites Complications table.

**Sacred Rites Complications**
| d4 | Complication |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | An unexpected situation came up causing you to perform the rite incorrectly. You feel that this has angered a god.
| 2 | A rite is interrupted when someone accuses you of performing it badly. Several people agree with that person's argument.
| 3 | Some of the materials you need to perform the rite have gone missing!
| 4 | Another priest insists you change the way you perform the rite, and the new method is very complicated and / or taxing.

## Pit Fighting
Pit fighting includes boxing, wrestling, and other nonlethal forms of combat in an organized setting with predetermined matches. If it involves a battle-to-the-death situation, the standard combat rules apply to that sort of activity.

***Cost.*** Engaging in this activity requires one workweek of effort from a character.

***Resolution.*** The character must make a series of checks, with a DC determined at random based on the quality of the opposition that the character runs into. A big part of the challenge in pit fighting lies in the unknown nature of a character's opponents.

The character makes three checks: Strength (Athletics), Dexterity (Acrobatics), and a special Constitution check that has a bonus equal to a roll of the character's largest Hit Die (this roll doesn't spend that die). If desired, the character can replace one of these skill checks with an attack roll using one of the character's weapons. The DC for each of the checks is 5 + 2d10; generate a separate DC for each one. Consult the Pit Fighting Results table to see how the character did.

**Pit Fighting Results**
| Result | Value |
| --- | --- |
| 0 successes | Lose your bouts, earning nothing. |
| 1 success | Win 50 GP. |
| 2 successes | Win 100 GP. |
| 3 successes | Win 200 GP. |

***Complications.*** Characters involved in pit fighting must deal with their opponents, the people who bet on matches, and the matches' promoters. Every workweek spent pit fighting brings a 10 percent chance of a complication, examples of which are on the Pit Fighting Complications table.

**Pit Fighting Complications**
| d6 | Complication |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | An opponent swears to take revenge on you. |
| 2 | A crime boss approaches you and offers to pay you to intentionally lose a few matches. |
| 3 | You defeat a popular local champion, drawing the crowd's ire. |
| 4 | You defeat a noble's servant, drawing the wrath of the noble's house. |
| 5 | You are accused of cheating. Whether the allegation is true or not, your reputation is tarnished. |
| 6 | You accidentally deliver a near-fatal wound to a foe. |

## Relaxation
Sometimes the best thing to do between adventures is relax. Whether a character wants a hard-earned vacation or needs to recover from injuries, relaxation is the ideal option for adventurers who need a break. This option is also ideal for players who don't want to make use of the downtime system.

***Cost.*** Relaxation requires one week. A character needs to maintain at least a modest lifestyle while relaxing to gain the benefit of the activity.

***Resolution.*** Characters who maintain at least a modest lifestyle while relaxing gain several benefits. While relaxing, a character gains advantage on saving throws to recover from long-acting diseases and poisons. In addition, at the end of the week, a character can end one effect that keeps the character from regaining hit points, or can restore one ability score that has been reduced to less than its normal value. This benefit cannot be used if the harmful effect was caused by a spell or some other magical effect with an ongoing duration.

***Complications.*** Relaxation rarely comes with complications.

## Religious Service
Characters with a religious bent might want to spend downtime in service to a temple, either by attending rites or by proselytizing in the community. Someone who undertakes this activity has a chance of winning the favor of the temple's leaders.

***Cost.*** Performing religious service requires access to, and often attendance at, a temple whose beliefs and ethos align with the character's. If such a place is available, the activity takes one workweek of time but involves no gold piece expenditure.

***Resolution.*** At the end of the required time, the character chooses to make either an Intelligence (Religion) check or a Charisma (Persuasion) check. The total of the check determines the benefits of service, as shown on the Religious Service Results table.

**Religious Service Results**
| Check Total | Result |
| --- | --- |
| 1—10 | No effect. Your efforts fail to make a lasting impression. |
| 11—20 | You earn one favor. |
| 21+ | You earn two favors. |

A favor, in broad terms, is a promise of future assistance from a representative of the temple. It can be expended to ask the temple for help in dealing with a specific problem, for general political or social support, or to reduce the cost of cleric spellcasting by 50 percent. A favor could also take the form of a deity's intervention, such as an omen, a vision, or a minor miracle provided at a key moment. This latter sort of favor is expended by the DM, who also determines its nature.

Favors earned need not be expended immediately, but only a certain number can be stored up. A character can have a maximum number of unused favors equal to 1 + the character's Charisma modifier (minimum of 1).

***Complications.*** Temples can be labyrinths of political and social scheming. Even the best-intentioned sect can fall prone to rivalries. A character who serves a temple risks becoming embroiled in such struggles. Every workweek spent in religious service brings a 10 percent chance of a complication, examples of which are on the Religious Service Complications table.

## Repairing an Item
Equipment and other items get damaged sometimes. You can pay someone to fix it for you, or you can fix it yourself if you'd like to save some coin.

***Cost.*** Characters can only repair an item if they have access to it and the item isn't completely destroyed. Additionally, it has to be possible for the item to be repaired. The DM determines whether or not it is possible for an item to be repaired. The other costs for repairing an item depend on whether a character is repairing it themselves or hiring someone to repair it for them.

If a character is repairing an item themselves, they need to pay half the cost in raw materials and time normally required to craft that item according to the rules for crafting in chapter 6 of the Player's Handbook (2024) and the rules for crafting magic items in chapter 7 of the Dungeon Master's Guide (2024). Additionally, the character needs to have access to and be proficient in the tools and skills required to craft the item as described by the crafting rules above. Multiple characters can work on repairing an item at the same time to decrease the time cost, also using the same crafting rules referenced above.

If a character is hiring someone else to repair the item for them, they need access to another that is willing and able to repair the item for them. It is usually easy to find such a character for common non-magical items in a city. The DM determines whether or not a suitable character is available. The hired character follows the same rules as above to repair the item since they are doing the repairs themselves. The client character must cover the cost of raw materials for the item, as well as pay the hired character 25 CP per hour spent on repairing the item. The client character can spend their time doing something else while the hired character works on repairing the item.

***Resolution.*** If the character repairs the item themselves, they spend the cost for raw materials and the time. Once the required time has elapsed, the item is repaired. If the character hires someone else to repair it for them, they pay the required cost in raw materials and labor then wait the required amount of time. At the end of that time period, the item is fully repaired and can be given back to the character.

***Complications.*** Repairing items rarely comes with complications.

## Research
Forewarned is forearmed. The research downtime activity allows a character to delve into lore concerning a monster, a location, a magic item, or some other particular topic to gain potentially useful information.

***Cost.*** Typically, a character needs access to a library or a sage to conduct research. Assuming such access is available, conducting research requires one workweek of effort and at least 50 GP spent on materials, bribes, gifts, and other expenses.

***Resolution.*** The character declares the focus of the research— a specific person, place, or thing. After one workweek, the character makes an Intelligence check with a +1 bonus per 50 GP spent beyond the initial 50 GP, to a maximum of +6. In addition, a character who has access to a particularly well-stocked library or knowledgeable sages gains advantage on this check. Determine how much lore a character learns using the Research Outcomes table.

**Research Outcomes**
| Check Total | Outcome |
| --- | --- |
| 1—5 | No effect. |
| 6—10 | You learn one piece of lore. |
| 11—20 | You learn two pieces of lore. |
| 21+ | You learn three pieces of lore. |

Each piece of lore is the equivalent of one true statement about a person, place, or thing. Examples include knowledge of a creature's resistances, the password needed to enter a sealed dungeon level, the spells commonly prepared by an order of wizards, and so on.

The DM decides exactly what a character learns. For a monster or an NPC, they can reveal elements of statistics or personality. For a location, they can reveal secrets about it, such as a hidden entrance, the answer to a riddle, or the nature of a creature that guards the place.

***Complications.*** The greatest risk in research is uncovering false information. Not all lore is accurate or truthful. A rival with a scholarly bent might try to lead the character astray, one of your sources may have lied, or you may have missed an important piece of context that alters the lore you obtained.

False information isn't the only kind of complication that can arise from research, however. Every workweek spent in research brings a 10 percent chance of a complication, examples of which are on the Research Complications table.

## Running a Business
Adventurers can try to earn an honest living by running their own business. Selling services and products can earn good profits, but it can also earn hard losses.

***Cost.*** To run a business, a character needs to have a business model that is approved by the DM. It has to make sense; businesses need to have the capital needed to operate the business, a way to buy necessary labor and supplies, and a way to exchange with customers. For example, if a character wants to run a farm, they need to have farmland (capital), be near a settlement where they can hire farmers (labor) and buy equipment and seeds (supplies), and have a marketplace or other businesses that they can sell to (exchange).

Running a business can be done for 1 workweek at a time. Characters running a business must also pay a maintenance cost to keep the business up and running every workweek. The character gets to choose how much this maintenance cost is. It must be at least 1 GP and the DM must determine that it is appropriate for the type of business being run. The maintenance cost determines the amount of potential reward and risk with this business. Higher maintenance costs mean bigger profits and losses.

***Resolution.*** At the end of each workweek, the player makes a d100 roll to determine how the business did financially. The Business Results table determines the outcome based on that roll based on the maintenance cost. If the revenue is lower than the maintenance cost, the player must pay the difference between the revenue and the maintenance cost. If the revenue is higher than the maintenance, then the player receives the difference instead. If a character can't afford to pay for a loss, they obtain a debt by the amount they didn't pay. Until they pay off that debt, the business receives a -10 penalty to all subsequent d100 rolls. The business receives another -10 penalty each time a new debt is incurred.

Businesses start out with a -5 penalty to the d100 roll, but characters can decrease the penalty and even turn it into a bonus by purchasing more capital to increase revenue. For example, characters could purchase advertisements to spread word of their business, purchase nicer furniture to make clients more comfortable, or buy a building in a wealthier part of town to move the business to. The Business Roll Modifier table shows the cost to increase the modifier for the d100 roll.

The DM can also grant a business circumstantial modifiers based on current events or actions characters take.

## Selling Items
Adventurers often end up with lots of items that they don't need. Sometimes, they run short on money and need to sell some of their possessions to be able to afford other things they want. Selling items is a great way for adventurers to get rid of gear they don't need and make some extra coin. It is a bit more difficult than buying items, however, since willing buyers can be difficult to find when you're not a known vendor.

Note that this activity is only for selling non-magical items. See the [Selling a Magic Item](#selling-a-magic-item) activity above for the rules of selling a magic item during downtime.

***Cost.*** Characters need access to a settlement to be able to sell items. There must be inhabitants in the settlement that are willing and able to purchase the items a character is trying to sell. The DM determines whether or not a character is able to sell certain items in a settlement.

Additionally, characters need a way to transport items they're selling so they can bring them to buyers. If the character doesn't have the carrying capacity to carry the items on their own, they need some other way to transport the items. Examples could include a wagon or another character helping to carry the items. The DM determines whether a character has the means to transport the items they plan on buying.

***Resolution.*** If a character is selling items that have a price listed in the Player's Handbook (2024) chapter 6 or other official D&D source material, they sell for the price listed in the source material. If an item is worn, used, or partially damaged, it sells for half the listed price. For example, a bedroll that has been on several adventures or a piece of armor that has taken at least 1 hit could be considered worn. A worn piece of equipment can be made to sell for full price if it is repaired through the [Repairing an Item](#repairing-an-item) activity. The DM determines whether or not a piece of equipment is worn or not.

The prices of items that don't have a price listing in any official D&D source material are determined by the DM. For items with multiple different price listings in D&D source material, the DM determines which price listing to use.

Once a character sells an item, a buyer takes that item and pays them the price determined by the rules above.

There is are limits to how many items a character can sell in a day. Each day a character tries to sell items, they can sell up to a maximum of (1d4 + the character's charisma modifier) x 50 GP worth of items, (1d4 + the character's charisma modifier) x 25 lbs worth of items, or 25 different types of items, whichever limit is met first. These maximums can't go below 0. The maximums are re-rolled each day per character.

If an item is worth more than the cost limit for the day or weighs more than the weight limit for the day, it will take multiple days to sell that item. If a character tries to sell an item that exceeds either of these limits, they need to keep track of a value called a sell timer to determine how many days it will take to sell the item. If the sell price of the item exceeds the maximum cost for a day, the sell timer is equal to that item's sell price. Otherwise, if the item exceeds the weight limit, the sell timer is equal to the weight of the item. If it exceeds both, the character should keep track of 2 separate sell timers for the item, each equal to the sell price and weight of the item respectively. Each day the character tries to sell that item, the sell timer(s) reduce by the maximum sell cost / weight for that day respective to the value they were derived from. Once all sell timers for an item meet or go below 0, the item sells. Trying to sell an item this way takes up the whole day each day it's attempted, including the day the item is sold.

For example, if a character tries to sell an item that is worth 300 GP and the maximum amount of GP they can sell for is 100 on the first day, they keep track of a sell timer that starts at 300 GP. They then subtract the maximum price for that day (100), and are left with a value of 200 GP for the sell timer. The next day they try to sell the item, the maximum price is 50 GP, which reduces the sell timer to 150 GP. The day after that, maximum price is 200 GP, which brings the sell timer to -50 GP. The item sells this day since the sell timer has gone below 0.

***Complications.*** Many interesting complications can arise from trying to sell items. Complications typically occur from disagreements with buyers, struggling to find a buyer, and troublemakers trying to take advantage of characters. Every day spent selling items brings a 2 percent chance of a complication, examples of which are on the Item Sale Complications table.

**Item Sale Complications**
| d8 | Complication |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | You don't have any luck with finding buyers. You don't receive any money from selling. If you were progressing toward selling an item that takes multiple days to sell, this day doesn't count toward your progress in selling that item. |
| 2 | The only kind of buyer you can find for the most expensive type of item you're selling is only willing to buy it for half price. If you're in the middle of trying to find a buyer for an item that takes multiple days to sell, this will be the offer you receive to sell the item once you've spent the required days looking for a buyer. After you've spent the required number of days to find a buyer, you can choose to restart the process to try and find a new buyer that may offer a better deal. |
| 3 | Rumors begin spreading that the items you're selling are stolen. Whether or not this is true is determined by how you obtained the items you've sold. |
| 4 | You let your guard down and thieves managed to steal all the items you were trying to sell while you weren't paying attention. |
| 5 | Two buyers fight over the most expensive items of the same type you're selling and break them in the process. If the items are worth 250 GP or less, they're completely destroyed. If it's an item worth more than 250 GP, its price is halved unless it's repaired. |
| 6 | The local authorities demand that you pay taxes on the money you earn from selling items. |
| 7 | A local vendor afraid you're taking away their business has come to harass you. |
| 8 | 3 small fiends in a trench coat disguised as a humanoid have come to buy all the items you're selling. |

## Shopping
Shopping is a common practice for adventurers. Spending gold on adventuring gear can be a great way for a character to increase their odds of survival. Characters can also buy services and non-adventuring-related items to improve their quality of life or give themselves a sense of luxury.

Note that this activity is only for buying non-magical items. See the [Buying a Magic Item](#buying-a-magic-item) activity above for the rules of buying a magic item during downtime.

***Cost.*** Characters need access to vendors that are selling the kinds of items they are looking to buy. Characters can almost always find the vendors they need for standard adventuring gear and tools in cities and towns. The DM determines whether or not those items are available to buy. The character will also need the funds to purchase the items they're attempting to buy. The costs of common items and services can be found in the Player's Handbook (2024) chapter 6. For any items not listed there or in other D&D source material, the DM determines the price. For items with multiple different price listings in D&D source material, the DM determines which price to use.

If the character doesn't have the carrying capacity to carry the items on their own, they need some other way to transport the items. ***Resolution.*** Once a character buys an item or service, they pay the required price and are given that item or service.

Characters can buy up to a maximum of 500 GP worth of items, or 50 different kinds of items in one day, whichever maximum is reached first. If an item costs more than 500 GP to buy, it takes 1 whole day to buy that item. 

***Complications.*** Minor inconveniences can occur now and then when shopping. They typically arise from the quality of the item or disputes with vendors and other customers. ## Sowing Rumors
Swaying public opinion can be an effective way to bring down a villain or elevate a friend. Spreading rumors is an efficient, if underhanded, way to accomplish that goal. Well-placed rumors can increase the subject's standing in a community or embroil someone in scandal.

***Cost.*** Sowing rumors costs an amount of time based on the size of the settlement it's being done in. The Sowing Rumors table shows the time requirements for each settlement size. The time spent must be consecutive; otherwise the rumor loses traction and dissipates. Additionally, it costs 5 GP per workweek to cover drinks, social appearances, and the like.

**Sowing Rumors**
| Settlement Size | Time Required |
| --- | --- |
| Village | 1 workweek |
| Town | 2 workweeks |
| City | 3 workweeks |

While this isn't an explicit requirement since it's not measurable, the rumor needs to be simple, concrete, and hard to disprove. An effective rumor also has to be believable, playing off what people want to believe about the person in question.

***Resolution.*** At the end of the time spent sowing the rumor, the character must make a DC 15 Charisma (Deception or Persuasion) check. If the check succeeds, the community's prevailing attitude toward the subject shifts one step toward friendly or hostile, as the character wishes. If the check fails, the rumor gains no traction, and further attempts to propagate it fail.

Shifting a community's general attitude toward a person or organization doesn't affect everyone in the community. Individuals might hold to their own opinions, particularly if they have personal experience in dealing with the subject of the rumors.

***Complications.*** Spreading rumors is very risky since you can't always know what people will do with the information they're given. Every workweek spent sowing rumors brings a 10 percent chance of a complication, examples of which are in the Sowing Rumors Complications table.

**Sowing Rumors Complications**
